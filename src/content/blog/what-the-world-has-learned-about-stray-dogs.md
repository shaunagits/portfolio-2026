Every community that has ever faced a stray dog problem has learned the same lesson, usually the hard way: you cannot round up, shelter, or remove your way out of it. The math of dog reproduction always wins. One unspayed female and her offspring can produce dozens of puppies in just a few years, which means any strategy that does not stop breeding at the source is expensive treading of water.

The good news is that we are no longer guessing. Over the past two decades, countries around the world have run what amounts to a global experiment in stray dog management. Some succeeded remarkably. Some failed painfully. Their results point clearly toward what works. And for an island like Oʻahu, where shelters have been over capacity for years and families along the Waiʻanae Coast see the problem in their own neighborhoods, those lessons apply directly.

---

## Bahrain: what happens when effort is not matched with scale

Bahrain is a useful starting point because it tried the right things, just not at the scale or with the sustained funding the problem demanded.

The Bahrain Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals launched a Catch, Neuter, Return campaign with Dogs Trust International in 2012. It was a five year effort budgeted at roughly $795,000, and it began with door to door surveys to count the stray population. Dogs were health checked, vaccinated, sterilized, and returned. The explicit goal was to stop breeding and reduce the number of dogs shot by police or killed on roads. A government backed trap, neuter, return effort followed in 2018, involving municipalities and private veterinary clinics.

Volunteers on the ground described the underlying weakness. Rescue groups operated with little to no government assistance, no public funds were allocated for spay and neuter, and organizations struggled just to feed an estimated 18,000 strays, relying on restaurant scraps and scarce donations. Sterilization capacity never came close to matching the reproduction rate. Years later, Bahrain was still spending significant sums annually on capture and management while the population kept growing.

The lesson: sterilization works as biology, but it only works as policy when it is funded, sustained, and sized to the actual population. Underfunded TNR is not a solution. It is a gesture.

## The Netherlands: the gold standard

The Netherlands is widely recognized as the first country to effectively eliminate its stray dog population, and it did so without euthanasia campaigns.

It did not start from a good place. In the 19th century the country had one of Europe's largest dog populations, and a rabies outbreak triggered mass abandonment. An early attempt at a dog tax backfired, pushing owners who could not pay to abandon even more animals. What ultimately worked was a coordinated, multi decade national strategy built on four pillars:

- **Government funded sterilization at national scale.** The countrywide CNVR program (Collect, Neuter, Vaccinate, Return) had municipal teams and shelters working together to sterilize and vaccinate free roaming dogs, with the government, not charities, paying the bill. World Animal Protection now points to this model as the most effective approach to stray dog management.
- **Making adoption the economic default.** Dutch municipalities imposed steep taxes on store bought and breeder purchased dogs, steering prospective owners toward shelters. Adoption demand emptied shelters, which freed capacity to absorb strays.
- **Real enforcement.** The 1962 Animal Protection Act set legal welfare standards, and abandonment and abuse now carry fines up to roughly €16,750 and potential prison time. The Netherlands even fields a dedicated animal police force that investigates cruelty and performs rescues.
- **Political representation.** The Party for the Animals keeps welfare on the national agenda permanently, not just during crises.

The lesson: no single tool did it. Sterilization stopped new strays from being born, adoption incentives emptied shelters, and enforcement stopped abandonment from refilling the streets. Every piece reinforced the others.

## Bhutan: proof that full coverage is actually achievable

If the Netherlands shows what a wealthy nation can do over decades, Bhutan shows what focused national will can accomplish fast, which arguably makes it the more relevant model for Hawaiʻi.

Bhutan partnered with Humane Society International starting in 2009, piloting high volume spay and neuter in the capital and training more than 35 Bhutanese veterinarians in high quality, high volume surgical techniques. Then, in March 2022, the King ordered a final nationwide push: the Nationwide Accelerated Dog Population Management and Rabies Control Programme. In under two years, at a cost of about $3.55 million, the country sterilized its entire free roaming dog population, the first nation ever to do so. Across the full project, more than 150,000 dogs were sterilized, roughly 95% of free roaming dogs were vaccinated against rabies, and 32,000 pet dogs were microchipped and registered.

The staffing model is the striking part. A total of 12,812 people were involved, including over 9,000 de-suups, community volunteers known as Guardians of Peace, who handled humane netting, transport, and logistics alongside veterinary professionals. Community engagement and public education were built into the program from the start.

The lesson: a small, geographically contained population, a trained high volume surgical corps, and a large organized volunteer force can achieve total sterilization coverage in a defined timeframe. Bhutan is a country of under 800,000 people. Oʻahu has about a million. The scale is comparable, and an island is even more contained than a Himalayan kingdom.

## Turkey: the cautionary tale

Turkey shows the cost of choosing removal over prevention. In July 2024, its parliament passed a law requiring municipalities to round up an estimated four million stray dogs into shelters, with euthanasia permitted for sick or aggressive animals. The problem: the country had only 322 shelters with capacity for about 105,000 dogs, roughly 2.5% of the stray population.

Critics dubbed it the "massacre law," predicting that dogs would either be killed through loopholes or warehoused in overcrowded, disease ridden facilities. Reports of mass killings at municipal shelters followed, mass protests erupted, and the country's Constitutional Court ultimately upheld the law over dissenting judges in late 2025. The underlying population problem remains unsolved.

The lesson: impoundment first policies without sterilization and without shelter capacity do not manage a population. They relocate suffering, at enormous financial and moral cost.

---

## The pattern across all four

Strip away the geography and the same formula appears everywhere success occurred. Sterilization at a scale that outpaces reproduction, funded primarily by government rather than donations. Vaccination and microchipping bundled into every surgery. Adoption pipelines and disincentives for breeding and buying that keep shelters flowing rather than filling. Enforcement against abandonment. And organized community volunteers multiplying what a small professional corps can accomplish.

Where any one of those pieces was missing, funding in Bahrain, prevention in Turkey, the problem persisted no matter how much money was spent on management.

## Bringing it home: Oʻahu's version of the problem

Oʻahu's situation is milder than Bahrain's or Turkey's, but the dynamics are identical and the trend line is concerning.

The Hawaiian Humane Society, Honolulu's contracted animal control authority, has reported being persistently over capacity for more than two years straight, with no point at which kennels sat empty. Statewide, shelter populations have stayed high as families struggle with the cost of living, pet friendly housing remains scarce, backyard breeders produce more animals than they can sell, and demand for low fee and no fee spay and neuter far outpaces available services. The nationwide veterinarian shortage hits an island state especially hard, and the traditional relief valve, transferring animals to mainland shelters, has closed because those shelters are full too. On the Waiʻanae Coast, loose and abandoned dogs have been a recognized community problem for over a decade.

In other words, Oʻahu is at the early Bahrain stage. The choice is whether to invest like the Netherlands and Bhutan now, or keep paying for crisis management indefinitely.

---

## 1. Get the city's new spay and neuter program funded right

This one is happening now. In July 2026, Honolulu's Department of Customer Services held public hearings on administrative rules for a new public spay and neuter program for dogs and cats, the successor framework to the long running Neuter Now and Feline Fix partnerships.

The central issue raised in testimony was reimbursement rates. Only nine clinics on the entire island currently participate, because the city's outdated rates do not cover the real cost of modern surgery, so clinics lose money on every voucher. The proposed rules would reimburse up to $450 per procedure and preserve $20 certificates for EBT, Medicaid, and housing assistance households. Residents can still weigh in by emailing CSD@honolulu.gov.

This is the single highest leverage local action available. The entire Dutch model rests on government funded sterilization, and Honolulu is writing those rules right now.

---

## 2. Take high volume surgery to the west side

Bhutan did not wait for dogs to come to clinics. It deployed teams.

Oʻahu's spay and neuter infrastructure is concentrated at Mōʻiliʻili and Hoʻopili, while much of the need is along the Waiʻanae Coast, where transportation is a genuine barrier. Regular mobile or pop up high volume clinics in Waiʻanae, Nānākuli, and Mākaha, modeled on the high quality, high volume protocols Humane Society International uses, would put sterilization where the puppies are actually being born.

---

## 3. Build our own de-suup corps

Bhutan's secret weapon was not money. It was 9,000 trained volunteers.

Oʻahu already has the raw material: rescue networks, foster communities, and groups like Paw Posse ʻOhana with tens of thousands of members reuniting lost pets. Formalizing that energy into a trained volunteer force covering humane handling, transport, trapping, recovery monitoring, and community education would multiply the output of every clinic day.

---

## 4. Enforce and leverage the laws we already have

Hawaiʻi has required microchipping of all dogs and cats since 2022, and Honolulu ordinance already mandates sterilization of repeatedly impounded strays and of all adopted animals.

The Netherlands shows that enforcement is what makes prevention stick. Pairing chip compliance outreach with accessible low cost surgery turns existing law into an actual population management tool rather than paper.

---

## 5. Attack the intake side: housing and abandonment

Shelter leaders across the state consistently name the same two drivers of surrender: cost of living and the scarcity of pet friendly rentals.

Advocacy for legislation encouraging landlords to accept pets, alongside pet food banks and wellness outreach that help families keep the animals they already have, shrinks the stream of dogs entering the system in the first place. Every surrender prevented is a kennel that stays open for a true stray.

---

## 6. Use the island advantage

Bhutan proved that a contained population can reach full sterilization coverage. Nothing swims to Oʻahu.

With a funded city program, west side mobile capacity, and an organized volunteer corps, total coverage of Oʻahu's free roaming and at risk dog population is not a fantasy. It is a project plan with a finish line. That is something almost no mainland city can say.

---

## The bottom line

Bahrain shows that good intentions without scale change nothing. Turkey shows that removal without prevention is cruelty with a budget line. The Netherlands and Bhutan show that the combination of publicly funded sterilization, community mobilization, enforcement, and adoption first culture does not just manage the problem. It ends it.

Oʻahu has the geography of Bhutan, the institutional foundations the Netherlands built on, and a rulemaking process open at this very moment. The question is not whether the playbook works. It is whether we run it.
